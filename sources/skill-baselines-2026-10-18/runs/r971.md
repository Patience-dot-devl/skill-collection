### Body

Every insert, update, and delete in your Postgres database is a signal that other systems may need. A search index has to reflect the new product price. A warehouse has to see the order that just shipped. A cache has to drop the stale record. The hard part is getting those signals out quickly, reliably, and without putting the source database at risk. Today we are announcing that the Ferrous Postgres change-data-capture (CDC) connector is generally available.

#### Sub-second latency, measured

In our benchmark, the connector delivered changes end to end with a p95 latency of 420 ms at a sustained rate of 20,000 rows per second. End to end means the time from a change being committed in Postgres to the moment it arrives at the destination.

We publish that number with its context. It comes from our own test setup. Your results will depend on network distance, the destination you write to, and the size of your rows. Before you commit to the connector, run the benchmark against a copy of your own workload.

Latency matters most when downstream systems make decisions on the data. A fraud check that runs on a delayed copy of an account balance is less useful than one that runs on a copy that is a fraction of a second behind. Sub-second delivery gives those systems a view of the database that is current enough for most operational use cases, without the batch windows and nightly loads that many teams still rely on.

#### No replication-slot bloat

Postgres logical replication tracks each consumer's position with a replication slot. A slot that stops advancing forces the server to keep write-ahead log (WAL) on disk. On a database where some tables change rarely, that WAL can grow for a long time, and the disk fills up at the worst possible moment.

The connector avoids this with slot heartbeats. It regularly confirms its position in the slot, even when no tracked tables are changing. Postgres can then recycle WAL that is no longer needed, and the slot does not quietly grow into an incident. You do not need a separate job that watches the WAL directory.

This matters most for teams with busy and quiet databases side by side. A consumer that only advances when a tracked table changes can leave a slot stuck for hours on a database that rarely writes. The heartbeat removes that dependency, so the same configuration behaves predictably whether the database is busy or idle.

#### Open source under Apache-2.0

The connector is open source under the Apache License 2.0. You can read the code, run it in your own infrastructure, modify it, and build it into commercial products, subject to the license terms. CDC sits between your primary database and everything downstream of it, so we think you should be able to see exactly what it does.

#### Run it yourself, or use Ferrous Cloud

Choose whichever fits your team. If you want full control, run the open-source connector in your own environment. If you would rather not operate it, the managed version runs on Ferrous Cloud. It is the same connector, with hosting and operations handled for you.

#### Get started

Read the quickstart, point the connector at your Postgres instance, and choose a destination. Start with one table and one destination, confirm that the latency and slot behaviour match your expectations, and then widen the scope. A staged rollout keeps the first run easy to reason about.

If something breaks, or your workload does not match our benchmark, please tell us. Real-world reports are the fastest way to make the connector better, and the open-source project is the right place to file them.

Try it on a non-production database this week, and let us know what you learn.
