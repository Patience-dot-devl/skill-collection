# CleanPlate Win-Back Sequence: Customers Who Cancelled in the Last 90 Days

## Assumptions

I made the following assumptions so that I could finish the sequence without asking questions. Change any of them if they are wrong.

- **Brand voice.** No brand guidelines were given, so the copy uses a warm, plain, slightly playful tone that suits a food brand. It is conversational and professional, and it does not guilt-trip anyone.
- **Exit survey data is stored on the customer record.** I assume each cancelled customer has a `cancel_reason` field (price, leftovers, skipping, other) that the email platform can read. The whole sequence depends on this field, because each email changes its main content block based on the reason.
- **The offer.** I used the full allowance: 30% off each of the next two boxes. It is applied automatically through a personal link, with a backup code (`WELCOMEBACK30`). It expires 9 days after Email 3 is sent. One A/B test below checks whether 20% works as well for customers who did not cancel because of price.
- **Half-portions.** I read "2-person half-portions" as a new portion size on the 2-person plan that gives smaller servings, so there is less food left over and, I assume, a lower box price. Where the copy mentions a price, I used `[half-portion price]` placeholders. If the half-portion box does not cost less, remove the price lines from the price-segment copy.
- **One-click skip.** I assume customers can skip a week with one click from the weekly menu email or from the account page, with no menus to navigate.
- **Trigger timing.** For new cancellations, the sequence starts 14 days after the cancellation date. This gives people some space and lets them miss a week or two of dinners first. For the existing 90-day backlog, enroll everyone at once but send in 3 waves over one week (oldest cancellations first) to protect sender reputation.
- **Prices, ratings and quotes are placeholders.** I did not invent customer counts, star ratings or testimonials. Every `[bracketed]` item must be filled with real data before sending.
- **Market.** I assume a US audience and US dollars. The copy works the same in other markets.

## Sequence Strategy

**Narrative arc: "You told us. We fixed it. Come see."** A standard win-back sequence opens by asking what went wrong. CleanPlate already knows, because 90% of cancellations gave a specific reason. So the sequence opens by naming that reason and showing the fix. This is the strongest message available, because two of the three main complaints (leftovers and skipping) now have direct product answers. The third (price) has a permanent partial answer (a cheaper half-portion box, if that is true) and a temporary one (the discount).

The emotional path runs from **being heard** (Email 1), to **curiosity and appetite** (Email 2), to **a reason to act now** (Emails 3 and 4), to **respect and an open door** (Email 5).

**Journey mapping**

| Email | Stage | Job |
|---|---|---|
| 1. We heard you | Re-awareness | Name their cancel reason and show the specific fix |
| 2. What is on the menu | Consideration | Make them hungry; show both new features working |
| 3. The offer | Decision | 30% off two boxes, with a deadline |
| 4. Last chance | Decision | Deadline reminder with lower friction |
| 5. Feedback and goodbye | Retention of goodwill | One-question survey; leave the door open |

**Escalation logic.** The sequence leads with the permanent product fixes and holds the discount back until Email 3. This is deliberate. A discount alone brings back price-sensitive customers for exactly two boxes, and then they cancel again when full price returns. If they come back because the product now fits their life (smaller portions, easy skipping), they are more likely to stay after box two. Urgency appears only in Emails 3 and 4, and the deadline is real.

**Success definition.** A customer has converted when they **reactivate their subscription and have a paid box scheduled**. They leave the sequence at once. The real success metric is one step further: **are they still active after box 3**, the first box at full price? (See Metrics to Track.)

## Branching Logic Notes

**Enrollment**
- Trigger: subscription status changed to cancelled 14 days ago.
- Backlog: everyone who cancelled 14 to 90 days ago, sent in 3 waves over one week, oldest cancellations first. Customers who cancelled less than 14 days ago enter through the normal trigger.

**Branching conditions**
- Email 1 content is chosen by `cancel_reason`. If the field is empty, use Variant D.
- A Variant D customer who clicks a reason button is retagged. Later emails use the new reason line.
- Clicked the CTA in Email 1 or 2 but did not reactivate → skip the rest of the warm-up and send Email 3 two days after the click.
- Did not open Email 3 → send Email 3b on Day 13.
- Clicked Email 3 but did not finish the restart → Email 4 uses the alternative opening.
- Open data is unreliable for many Apple Mail users because of Mail Privacy Protection, which loads images automatically. Base the important branches (early offer, 3b) on clicks where possible. If the platform can flag machine opens, exclude them from the "opened" condition.

**Exit conditions**
- **Converted:** the customer reactivated and has a paid box scheduled. Remove them at once, even in the middle of a wait step, and move them into a "returning customer" onboarding flow (see Next Steps).
- Unsubscribed or marked an email as spam.
- Email 5 sent (sequence complete).

**Re-entry rules**
- A customer who reactivates and then cancels again can re-enter after **at least 12 months** from their last win-back offer. Before that, they get the sequence without a discount (Emails 1, 2 and 5 only).
- Never send the 30% offer to the same customer twice in 12 months. Otherwise, regular customers learn that cancelling earns a discount.

**Suppression rules**
- Do not send to customers who unsubscribed from marketing email.
- Do not send if the customer is already in another active automated sequence (for example, a referral or survey flow).
- Do not send if the customer contacted support in the last 48 hours. Hold them and resume afterwards.
- **Permanent suppression:** customers who cancelled because of a food-safety or quality complaint, customers with a chargeback or payment dispute, and customers who moved outside your delivery area. Send these customers to customer service, not marketing.
- Hold out **10% of the enrolled list at random** as a control group that gets no win-back email. Without this, you cannot tell how many customers came back because of the sequence and how many would have come back anyway.

## A/B Test Suggestions

**1. Discount depth for customers who did not leave over price (20% versus 30%)**
- *What:* Customers in the leftovers, skipping and other segments receive either 20% off or 30% off two boxes. The price segment always gets 30%.
- *Why:* These customers left because of the product, which is now fixed. They may come back for a smaller discount, and that saves margin on about 55% of the list.
- *Split:* 50/50, random, within each segment.
- *Winner:* Highest **net revenue per enrolled customer after 90 days**. This includes discount cost and box-3 retention. Do not pick the winner on reactivation rate alone.

**2. Email 1 subject line: reason named versus benefit named**
- *What:* "You said too many leftovers. Fair." against "Dinner for two, without the leftovers" (and the matching pair for the price and skipping variants).
- *Why:* Naming the customer's own complaint may feel personal and get more opens, or it may feel like being watched. This test shows which works for your audience.
- *Split:* 50/50 within each reason segment.
- *Winner:* **Click rate** (not open rate, which Apple Mail Privacy Protection distorts). Wait at least 3 days before you decide.

**3. Timing of the offer: Email 3 on Day 10 versus offer in Email 1**
- *What:* The control holds the offer until Email 3. The test variant adds the 30% offer to Email 1, so the price segment sees the fix and the discount together.
- *Why:* This checks the main assumption of this sequence, which is that leading with the product fix produces customers who stay longer. It is most worth testing in the price segment.
- *Split:* 50/50 within the price segment only.
- *Winner:* **Box-3 retention** (the share of reactivated customers still active after the first full-price box), with reactivation rate as a second check.

Run one test per segment at a time, and make sure each arm has enough customers to show a real difference. With a reactivation rate of a few percent, you need several thousand customers per arm to detect small differences. If the list is smaller, run test 1 first, because it has the largest effect on margin.

## Metrics to Track

**Primary conversion metric:** **Incremental reactivations**. This is the reactivation rate of the enrolled group minus the reactivation rate of the holdout group, within 30 days of Email 1.

**Quality metric (track alongside the primary metric):** **Box-3 retention**, which is the share of reactivated customers who receive and pay for their first full-price box. If many customers leave after the two discounted boxes, the sequence is buying temporary revenue, not winning customers back.

**Per-email metrics:** open rate (trend only), click-through rate, click-to-reactivation rate, unsubscribe rate and spam complaint rate.

**Per-segment metrics:** Report all of the above by `cancel_reason`. You should expect the leftovers and skipping segments to reactivate at a higher rate than the price segment, because their problem has a permanent fix. If this is not the case, the product fix is not convincing in the copy, or the feature does not solve the problem as well as hoped.

**Sequence-level metrics:**
- Overall reactivation rate and incremental lift against the holdout
- Time to reactivation (which email drove it)
- Drop-off points (the email after which most customers stop opening or clicking)
- Plan mix of returning customers (half-portion against standard), which shows whether half-portions are the real draw
- Net revenue per enrolled customer after 90 days, after discount cost
- Email 5 survey answers, grouped and fed back to the product team

**Review cadence:** Review weekly for the first month (the backlog waves will produce data quickly). After that, review monthly. Review the box-3 retention figures at around 6 weeks, after the first reactivated customers have received a full-price box.

## Setup Checklist (Any Email Platform)

1. Make sure `cancel_reason` from the exit survey is synced to the customer profile in your email platform, with the values price, leftovers, skipping and other.
2. Create the personal offer link (discount applied automatically to the next two boxes, with a 9-day expiry from the Email 3 send date) and the backup code `WELCOMEBACK30`, limited to one use per customer.
3. Create the automation, with the trigger "cancelled 14 days ago". Create a separate one-time enrollment for the 14 to 90 day backlog, sent in 3 waves.
4. Add the 10% random holdout split immediately after enrollment.
5. Add the suppression filters: unsubscribed, in another active flow, support contact in the last 48 hours, quality complaint, chargeback, outside delivery area, and win-back offer redeemed in the last 12 months.
6. Add Email 1 with a conditional split on `cancel_reason` (4 content variants) and the retagging links for Variant D.
7. Add Emails 2, 3, 3b, 4 and 5 with the delays in the overview table, and the click-based early path to Email 3.
8. Set the exit condition "subscription reactivated" so that it removes the customer from the flow immediately, even during a wait step.
9. Fill every `[bracketed]` placeholder with real prices, recipes, dates and a real customer quote. Ask legal to check the after-discount price wording.
10. Set up tracking: UTM parameters on every link, reactivation attribution by email, the holdout comparison and a box-3 retention report.
11. Send test emails for each `cancel_reason` variant and check the personal offer link on mobile before the first wave.
