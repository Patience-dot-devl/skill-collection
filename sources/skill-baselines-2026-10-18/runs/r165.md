# Ferrous: 8-week lead-generation plan for the Postgres CDC connector

**Window:** Monday 26 October to Sunday 20 December 2026, with a preparation week starting 19 October
**Budget:** EUR 40,000
**Requested target:** 300 sales-qualified leads (SQLs)

## The short version

I would not commit to 300 SQLs on this budget and timeline. EUR 40,000 for 300 SQLs works out to about EUR 133 per SQL. Infrastructure software aimed at data-platform leads normally costs several hundred euros or more per SQL. The bigger constraint is people, though: 300 SQLs in eight weeks means roughly 38 discovery calls a week, every week, at a 20-person company. My realistic range for this plan is **130 to 190 SQLs**. I would set **150 as the committed number and 200 as the stretch goal**. The last section explains what it would take to reach 300.

The plan is built around your strongest asset, which is not ad spend. It is the 9,000 people who have already starred your open-source repo, together with the issue authors, contributors and users behind them. Most of those people work somewhere. Some of those companies have 200 to 2,000 employees and run Postgres in production. Those people already trust your engineering. So the main engine of this push is turning that existing open-source signal into sales conversations. The launch, the technical content, the events and a modest paid budget all exist to feed that engine and to widen it.

## Assumptions

I have not asked any questions, so these are the assumptions behind the plan. Please correct any that are wrong, because several of them change the numbers.

- Ferrous is based in Europe and sells into Europe and North America. The budget is in euros, so I have weighted events towards Europe.
- The connector is new but works in production. You can show at least one design partner or early user, even anonymously.
- The connector is commercial, or it has a commercial tier: a managed version, support, or enterprise features. If it is fully open source with no paid path, "SQL" needs a different definition.
- The likely alternatives in your buyers' minds are self-run Debezium on Kafka, managed ELT tools such as Fivetran or Airbyte, and native cloud-vendor replication. The messaging below is written against those.
- Sales capacity is two or three people who can take first calls (founders, account executives or sales engineers). Your engineers can give about one day a week, in total, to content and community work.
- An SQL means: a person at a company with 200 to 2,000 employees, in a data-platform, data-engineering or infrastructure leadership role (or directly reporting to one), who runs Postgres in production, has a concrete CDC use case (to a warehouse, a lake or a stream), and has a timeline within two quarters. **Sales confirms all of this on a discovery call before the lead counts.** A form fill or a webinar sign-up is not an SQL.
- You have basic product analytics or a signup flow that can tell you which companies are trying the connector. If you do not, setting that up is a preparation-week task.

## Why 300 is the wrong commitment, in numbers

A typical B2B infrastructure funnel converts 15 to 25 percent of marketing-qualified leads into SQLs. To get 300 SQLs, you would need about 1,200 to 2,000 genuine, in-profile hand-raisers in eight weeks. At current LinkedIn costs for a narrow, senior, technical audience, EUR 40,000 spent only on paid lead generation would buy perhaps 250 to 400 leads. Those would give you 40 to 80 SQLs. Paid media alone cannot get you there. Warm open-source signals convert much better, but the pool of them is finite.

Sales capacity is the second limit. If three people each take 10 to 12 first calls a week, they can handle about 30 to 36 a week at most. That is with everything else they do pushed aside. December also loses at least one effective week to the holidays. Even if marketing produced 300 qualified people, the team could not run 300 proper discovery calls in this window.

## Strategy: five engines, in order of expected yield

### 1. Open-source signal to pipeline (expected 50 to 70 SQLs)

This is the core of the plan.

- **Build the account list (preparation week).** Export your stargazers, forkers, issue and discussion authors, contributors, and Docker or package download sources where you can see them. Enrich them with a signal tool (Common Room, Clay or similar) to find each person's company and role. Filter to companies with 200 to 2,000 employees that use Postgres. My rough estimate is 40 percent identifiable, and a quarter of those in profile, which gives about 800 to 1,000 people at 400 to 600 accounts. Rank the accounts by signal strength. An open issue or a recent clone counts for more than a star from three years ago.
- **Warm, engineer-led outreach in three waves (weeks 1, 3 and 6).** Messages come from a named engineer or founder, not from a sequencer persona. They mention the person's real interaction with the repo. They offer something useful: early access to the connector, a 30-minute "CDC architecture review" of their current setup, or the benchmark report. Expect a 6 to 10 percent positive reply rate on warm signals. Expect less than half that on cold contacts.
- **Capture intent inside the repo itself.** Add a clear connector section and call to action to the README, the docs and the release notes. Add an optional "talk to an engineer" link in the CLI or console output for the connector, if your community will accept it. Put a short, honest note in the GitHub discussion forum.
- **Run a weekly "who is trying it" review.** Every Monday, sales and one engineer look at new signups, connector installs and repo activity from in-profile accounts, and pick who to contact that week.

### 2. Launch and technical content (expected 25 to 40 SQLs)

Data-platform leads do not respond to marketing copy. They respond to evidence that you understand the hard parts of Postgres CDC.

- **One flagship asset: a reproducible benchmark and engineering write-up.** Cover throughput and latency, WAL retention and replication-slot behaviour under load, schema-change handling, initial snapshot time, and recovery after failover. Compare it honestly with self-run Debezium and with one managed tool. Publish the harness so readers can run it themselves. Hire a freelance technical writer and designer to package it; your engineers supply the substance.
- **A launch sequence in week 2:** a "Show HN" post, the blog post, a Postgres mailing-list and community post where appropriate, a LinkedIn post from the founders and engineers, and the first newsletter sponsorship.
- **Three supporting pieces (weeks 3 to 7):** a migration guide ("Moving from Debezium to Ferrous without re-snapshotting"), a short "how we handle replication slots so you don't page at 3 a.m." post, and a customer or design-partner story.
- **A gated item only where it earns the gate.** Keep the benchmark ungated so it spreads. Put a gate on a "CDC readiness checklist and architecture review" offer, which works as a hand-raise.

### 3. Live technical sessions and small events (expected 20 to 30 SQLs)

- **One online workshop in week 4:** "Zero-downtime Postgres CDC in production". It is run by your engineers, with a live demo, and follow-up is booked during the session. Aim for 250 registrations and 100 live attendees.
- **One partner session in week 7,** co-hosted with a warehouse, streaming or managed-Postgres vendor whose customers need CDC. They bring the audience; you bring the content.
- **Two or three hosted dinners or meetups for 15 to 20 data-platform leads each,** in your strongest European cities. Good candidates are Amsterdam, Berlin and London; choose by your account density. Invite from the signal list. Each one should be a technical conversation, not a pitch.
- **AWS re:Invent (30 November to 4 December, Las Vegas) falls in week 6.** A booth is not worth it on this budget. If you have 15 or more priority US accounts, an invite-only dinner there is a good use of about EUR 4,000. If you do not, put that money into the European dinners.
- **PGConf.EU (20 to 23 October, Valencia) has just ended.** If anyone from Ferrous attended or spoke, follow up with every contact you met in week 1. Those conversations go cold quickly.

### 4. Targeted paid media (expected 15 to 30 SQLs)

- **LinkedIn:** campaigns aimed at the ranked account list plus job titles (head of data platform, data engineering manager, staff data engineer, platform engineering lead). Use document ads that carry the benchmark summary, and retarget visitors to the benchmark and docs pages. Do not run broad interest targeting.
- **Newsletter sponsorships:** two or three placements in newsletters your buyers actually read, such as Postgres Weekly and Data Engineering Weekly. Check current rates and audience fit before you book. Point them at the benchmark, not at a demo form.
- **No broad search campaigns.** Bid only on a small set of high-intent terms, such as "Postgres CDC", "Debezium alternative" and "logical replication to Snowflake/BigQuery". Cap that spend and stop it at the week-3 checkpoint if it is not producing qualified traffic.

### 5. Existing network, partners and referrals (expected 15 to 25 SQLs)

- Ask current customers and design partners for one introduction each to a peer at another company. Offer a reason that suits engineers, such as early access to a feature or a joint talk.
- Agree on referral or co-selling terms with one or two technology partners from engine 3.
- Re-engage closed-lost and stalled opportunities from the last 12 months where CDC was part of the conversation.

**Total expected range: about 125 to 195 SQLs.** That is the source of the 150 committed and 200 stretch figures.

## Measurement and decision rules

**Weekly dashboard,** reviewed every Monday by marketing and sales together:

- SQLs by source engine and by week, against a running target of about 19 a week. Expect a slow start and a higher rate from week 3.
- Leading indicators: accounts reached, positive reply rate, workshop registrations, benchmark page visits from in-profile accounts, connector installs and signups from in-profile accounts.
- Cost per SQL by paid channel.
- Speed to first contact for hand-raisers. The goal is the same business day.

**Checkpoint 1 (end of week 3):** If cumulative SQLs are below 30, find out whether the cause is reach (too few contacts), message (contacts but no replies) or qualification (meetings that do not qualify), and fix that first. Stop any paid channel whose cost per SQL is above EUR 1,000 or that has produced no SQLs at all. Move the money to the open-source outreach and the dinners.

**Checkpoint 2 (end of week 5):** Release the reserve to the channel with the lowest cost per SQL that still has room to scale. Reset the forecast for weeks 6 to 8 and tell leadership the expected final number. Do not let that number arrive as a surprise in week 8.

**Quality check:** Each week, sales scores a sample of SQLs. If more than 20 percent fail the SQL definition after the discovery call, tighten the qualification before the call rather than lowering the bar to hit the number.

## Risks and how to handle them

- **Community backlash at a commercial push.** Open-source users react badly to aggressive selling. Keep outreach personal and technical, coming from engineers. Never contact people based on private data. Make the open-source project visibly better during the same weeks, for example with fixes and docs, so the community can see you are still investing in it.
- **Sales becomes the bottleneck.** If more than 30 first calls a week are booked, have a sales engineer or senior engineer take the technical first calls. Or offer a group "office hours" session for earlier-stage interest.
- **The benchmark is late.** It is the anchor for the content, the paid media and the outreach. Start it in the preparation week and keep the scope small. If it slips, launch with the architecture-review offer and the migration guide, and publish the benchmark in week 3.
- **December slowdown.** Front-load activity. Weeks 7 and 8 should aim to book January meetings, not to squeeze out more December SQLs.
- **Privacy and consent.** Enrichment and outreach in Europe must follow GDPR. Use legitimate-interest assessments, give a clear opt-out in every message, and do not scrape private data. Check this with whoever handles legal before wave 1.

## If 300 SQLs is a firm requirement

Three ways to get closer, in order of preference:

1. **Extend the window to 12 to 14 weeks.** Run the push into Q1, when budgets reset and the open-source outreach can reach more accounts. On the same EUR 40,000, this alone could lift the result to about 220 to 260.
2. **Raise the budget to about EUR 90,000 to 120,000** and add sales capacity: one contract sales-development rep and one sales engineer for the period. Without extra capacity, more budget mostly produces leads that nobody follows up in time.
3. **Change the metric.** Count 300 "qualified conversations", meaning in-profile hand-raisers who have engaged with sales. Keep the stricter SQL count as a separate number. This is honest only if leadership agrees to the change in advance.
